# The GamingCouch recipe

**Small games. Big reactions. One more round.**

GamingCouch brings people together through short action party games, mixed into playlists. Your game contributes one memorable moment to the evening: a narrow escape, a perfectly timed shove, or a last-second comeback that gets everyone shouting at the screen.

**Read this before requesting developer tools access.** It explains the kinds of games we want to build together and helps you decide whether your idea fits.

## Start with one great minute

Imagine a group of friends launching your game for the first time. Some play games every day; others rarely pick up a controller. They should understand what to do, start affecting each other, and reach an exciting finish in about a minute.

That is the GamingCouch recipe. Build around one clear idea, with controls people can learn in a round. Let the players create the surprises.

The playlist provides variety across the evening, so your game can stay focused. It does not need a campaign, an unlock system, or enough content to fill an entire game night on its own. A small idea, executed well, is exactly what we are looking for.

## The ingredients

| Ingredient | What to aim for |
| --- | --- |
| **2–8 players** | Shared-screen action that stays fun and readable across your supported player counts. |
| **Bot opponents (recommended)** | Let people try your game and get a feel for it before gathering a larger party. |
| **One of four controller presets** | Pick a ready-made layout that fits your game, and keep its actions easy to learn. |
| **Roughly one-minute rounds** | A quick start, rising pressure, and a clear finish. Each round is a fresh start. |
| **Meaningful player interaction** | Players directly influence each other's choices and results. |
| **A little chaos** | Enough uncertainty for surprises and comebacks, while keeping actions and outcomes understandable. |

### Give everyone room to play

Aim to support two to eight players where your idea allows it. Games for fewer players can fit, but fewer groups will be able to include them in a playlist.

Test the smallest and largest groups you support. A spacious arena may feel empty with two players and overwhelming with eight. Adjust arena size, hazards, or available space when that helps preserve the same fun at different player counts.

Design for a shared 16:9 screen. Players should be able to find themselves and read the action from the couch, even when things get hectic.

### Make room for bot opponents

Ideally, include bot opponents so someone can jump in and try your game without waiting for a full group. Games with bot support are much more likely to be played and tested before being brought to a larger party. That first session gives someone a reason to say, “We should play this with friends.”

Keep the bots simple and focused on the core interaction. They should create opportunities to learn the game, compete, and have fun. Bot support also makes it easier to test your own game while you develop it.

### Make the controls easy to learn

Keep the goal and the controls simple enough to grasp during the first round. Movement plus one action can already produce plenty of interesting decisions.

GamingCouch currently offers four ready-made controller presets in devspace:

| Controller preset | Available controls |
| --- | --- |
| **Stick, Primary and Secondary** | A directional stick, a primary action button, and a secondary action button. |
| **Stick, Primary** | A directional stick and a primary action button. |
| **Buttons Left/Right** | Two buttons: left and right. |
| **Buttons Left/Right/Primary action** | Three buttons: left, right, and a primary action. |

Choose the simplest preset that supports your core interaction. You select a controller for each minigame, so different minigames can use different presets, and you can change your selection later.

Design around the controls in your chosen preset. You do not need to use every available action; if your idea requires more controls, simplify it. Keep the game comfortable to play with both gamepads and phone controllers.

Tap-and-hold actions can work when they feel natural, but hiding more moves behind combinations or button modes still adds complexity. If your concept needs a long control explanation, simplify the concept.

Players can improve their timing, positioning, and tactics over repeated rounds. They should have fun before they become good at it.

### Build toward a finish

Aim for rounds of around one minute. This is a pacing target, not an exact stopwatch rule: some rounds will end sooner, and others may run a little longer.

Give the game a way to reach a conclusion. A shrinking arena, disappearing platforms, increasing danger, or a finish line can bring players into a decisive final encounter. Use a timer when it serves the idea; consider whether the gameplay itself could create a more satisfying ending.

Avoid stalemates and long waits for eliminated players. If most rounds end before newcomers understand what happened, give the opening a little more breathing room.

### Make the players matter to each other

Interaction is the heart of the recipe. Players might push each other, block a route, steal an objective, or compete over a ball that everyone can affect.

A useful question is: **Would my choices change if the other players were removed?** If everyone is mainly completing a solo challenge beside each other, look for a stronger way to connect them.

Interaction also lets a group challenge a strong player. A temporary alliance or a well-timed interruption can turn a predictable result into a story worth retelling.

### Season the chaos carefully

Several people competing in the same space already create unpredictability. Start there before adding random events.

If you add hazards, power-ups, or changing conditions, give players enough information to react. Surprises should create opportunities for play. When nobody can tell why they won or lost, reduce the chaos.

## Keep the scope small

These examples show the scale we mean. They are starting points for your own ideas.

| A focused concept | Scope that takes it away from the recipe |
| --- | --- |
| Push rivals out of a shrinking arena using movement and one shove button. | Character classes, equipment builds, and a long list of combat abilities. |
| Scramble for a ball and knock it into a goal. | A full sports simulation with detailed team management and complex controls. |
| Race across collapsing platforms while blocking each other's routes. | A platforming adventure with exploration, checkpoints, and long levels. |
| Steal and hold a shared object while everyone chases its carrier. | An economy with crafting, inventory management, and upgrades between rounds. |

Our focus is **action party games**. Long strategy games, quizzes, story-driven adventures, and games built mainly around individual puzzle-solving fall outside that focus. A light puzzle element can work inside an action game, provided the rules stay quick to learn and interaction between players remains central.

If your idea depends on lengthy tutorials, persistent progression, or several minutes of setup before the fun starts, reshape it around its smallest playable moment. Discuss uncertain cases with us before investing too much time in the design.

## Focus your effort on the game

GamingCouch provides the surrounding party experience: menus, playlist flow, scoreboards, transitions, controller support, and a shared player HUD. Design around the platform's music rather than a separate game soundtrack.

Your focus is the gameplay: movement, interactions, rules, visuals, sound effects, and a clear result at the end of each round. Make that short experience feel great.

Start with a prototype you could reasonably explore in a weekend. That is a scope guide, not a deadline or a promise that a finished game takes two days. A polished release still needs testing and refinement.

If you are new to game development, modifying an example can help you get started. If you are experienced, bring that experience to the feel and clarity of a small game. In both cases, prove the central interaction before adding more game mechanics.

## Taste, test, and adjust

Put the prototype in front of friends, including people who do not play many games. Give them a short explanation, then watch a few rounds. If your game supports bots, try it with one person playing against bots as well as a mix of friends and bots.

- Do they understand the goal and controls in the first round?
- Do they react to each other, change tactics, and create unexpected moments?
- Does the round reach a satisfying finish in about a minute?
- Can everyone follow the action at each supported player count?
- Are eliminated players back in the action soon enough?
- Do they want another round?

When something falls flat, first try removing a rule, improving feedback, or strengthening the interaction. Add a mechanic when it solves a clear problem.

## Checklist before you start development

A few questions to shape your first prototype:

- What will players do, and how will they affect each other?
- Can newcomers pick up the goal and controls in a round?
- What brings the round to an exciting finish in about a minute?
- How many people can play?

Start with the moment you want players to laugh, compete, or call for a rematch, and build from there. We'd love to see what you come up with.
